Position
Statement

I have spent three years building AI systems that tell people what they are. Truescore tells you whether your interview answer was good. Niramoy tells a doctor whether the symptom they typed should be standardised differently. Kellafotey tells a hiring manager what kind of candidate they just watched.

The hard problem in each of these is not the model. It is the moment of acceptance — the half-second in which a person looks at an AI's output and decides whether to believe it, override it, or ignore it. That moment is a media problem as much as an algorithmic one. It is governed by latency, by visual hierarchy, by typographic restraint, by how confidence is shown without being claimed.

I am applying to Bremen because I want to study this question with the time, methods, and disciplinary rigour that industry has not allowed me. The portfolio that follows is the work I have made while learning to recognise the question.

01 · FLAGSHIP CASE STUDY · 2025–PRESENT

Truescore.

A feedback engine I built alone. Three modules, one architectural rule: nothing leaves the device.

Role
Founder · Designer · Sole engineer
Year
2025 — present
Live
truescore.tech
Stack
Next.js 14 TypeScript Tailwind Framer Motion Gemini API Groq API Web Speech API PDF.js Vercel

The Question

Job seekers in Bangladesh have access to LeetCode-style coding tools and Glassdoor-style company gossip, but almost nothing in the middle: structured, honest, immediate feedback on how they actually performed in an interview. The platforms that exist either upload your data to opaque servers or charge subscriptions at developed-market prices. I wanted to know whether a single-developer, privacy-first, browser-based tool could fill that gap.

The System

Truescore is three integrated modules sharing one architectural rule. AI Mock Interview uses live voice recognition to capture spoken answers, then an LLM scores each response across five evaluation metrics with verbatim feedback. Resume Analyzer ingests a PDF or DOCX, extracts text locally, and returns a multi-dimensional grade — content (40%), formatting (25%), keywords (20%), experience relevance (15%) — on an A–F scale. ATS Resume Maker rewrites uploaded resumes into ATS-optimised formats and exports to PDF, Word, or plain text.

The architectural rule is that all parsing happens client-side. A user's resume never reaches my servers. Only the abstracted feature vector — never the document — is sent to the LLM API. This is unusual for the genre, and it is what makes the product usable for users I care about: people who would not, and should not, hand their CV to an opaque cloud service.

Design Decisions

I chose a cyberpunk visual language against the genre default of corporate blue — neon magenta and cyan against deep ink, monospace headers, deliberate visual maximalism on the landing page that resolves into restraint inside the actual analysis modules. The reasoning is functional, not just aesthetic: the modules themselves require concentration, so the marketing surface absorbs all the visual energy and the work surface gets out of the way.

Loading states are first-class design objects. When the LLM is processing a resume, the user is not staring at a generic spinner — they see the actual stages of the analysis (parsing, keyword extraction, scoring, generating recommendations) with real-time progress indicators. The waiting time is not hidden; it is narrated. I learned in user testing that this turned anxious waiting into engaged anticipation.

The grade output uses an A–F scale rather than a 0–100 score. A 73/100 reads as a failing interview answer to most users; a B− reads as "you're close, here's how to clear the gap." The same numerical assessment, dressed differently, produced measurably different emotional responses in pilot users.

Technical Approach

The frontend is Next.js 14 with TypeScript and Tailwind, deployed on Vercel. The PDF parsing uses PDF.js running in the browser with a worker thread; DOCX parsing uses a library running in the browser; voice capture uses the Web Speech API for transcription with a custom debounce on confidence scores. Animations are Framer Motion with carefully scoped layout transitions — I use them for state changes, not decoration.

The most interesting engineering problem was structuring the prompt for consistent multi-dimensional scoring. LLMs are notoriously inconsistent at numeric outputs; ask Gemini for a score 0–100 and you'll get wild variance between runs. I solved this by reframing the prompt as a structured rubric — giving the model explicit anchors at each grade level, asking for evidence-based reasoning before the score, and requiring the score to be derivable from the reasoning. Inter-run variance dropped from ±18 points to ±4.

What it Taught Me

Building Truescore alone — handling product, design, frontend, backend, prompt engineering, and deployment — taught me what your programme description names directly: that programming is the central skill that allows one to "create your own media without the bonds of existing software tools." I could not have built a privacy-first architecture using a tool like Bubble or Webflow. The thing the product is — its position — is bound up with the fact that I wrote the parsing code myself.

What I am missing, and what I want from a master's, is methodology. I built Truescore through user testing and intuition. I would like to build the next thing through user studies, formal evaluation, and a literature.

02 · FLAGSHIP CASE STUDY · 2025–PRESENT

The CC Grammarly layer.

An interaction pattern, designed for clinicians, that has to earn trust in under three hundred milliseconds.

Project
Niramoy · AI healthcare platform · Celloscope Ltd
Role
AI Product Manager · Interaction designer
Year
2025 — present
Languages
Bengali · Hindi · English (clinical)
Status
MVP · Pilot deployment

The Question

A physician in Bangladesh sees forty patients a day. She types fast, she types in mixed Bengali-English, she does not have time to look up standardised symptom terminology in a 45,000-entry medical vocabulary. But the prescription that leaves her clinic enters a chain — pharmacist, insurer, public health record — that needs the standardised term. The naive solution is to constrain her input to a dropdown. The right solution is to let her type freely and quietly do the work in the background.

How do you build a real-time AI suggestion layer that helps a physician without ever interrupting her? How do you preserve clinician agency while still nudging toward consistency? These are the questions the CC layer was designed to answer.

The System

Every symptom typed into the Chief Complaint section flows through what we internally call the CC Grammarly layer. Two paths exist. Path A — Structured selection: the physician types, semantic search finds matches in the 45,000-entry database in under 300 ms, a dropdown appears, she selects one, the term is marked VERIFIED with a database mapping ID. Path B — Free text input: she types something idiosyncratic ("talks without thinking"), the term is captured raw and an LLM mapping engine runs in the background, returning a suggestion within 2 seconds: "Do you mean Logorrhea?" She can accept, reject, or edit. If she edits, the AI re-triggers.

Design Decisions

The accept/reject pair is symmetric in size and visual weight. Most AI suggestion patterns I have seen visually privilege the AI's recommendation — a brightly-coloured accept button next to a muted reject. This subtly biases the user toward the model. We made both buttons identical and placed them side by side. The doctor's choice is structurally treated as the doctor's choice.

Verified vs unverified status is visually quiet. A small checkmark, an amber dot. We considered a more prominent indicator and rejected it: when forty patients an hour are flowing through the screen, you do not want bright colours competing for attention. The status is there for downstream systems, not for performance.

The original input is always preserved. Even when a term is accepted as a standardised mapping, the doctor's original phrase is stored alongside it. This is partly an audit-trail concern, but mainly a respect concern: the doctor's words are not erased by the system's words.

The design problem was not the model. It was the moment the doctor decided whether to trust it.

Technical Approach

Semantic search runs against an indexed Postgres table of 45,000 medical terms with synonym handling. The LLM mapping uses a structured prompt that returns the closest standardised term plus a confidence score; mappings below threshold are marked low-confidence and shown to the doctor with an explicit warning rather than silently accepted. Every action is logged with timestamp and physician ID for audit. The non-functional requirements I set were aggressive: 300 ms search, 200 ms box creation, 2 s AI mapping ceiling. We hit them.

What it Taught Me

Niramoy taught me that HCI is not a layer on top of a working system; it is the system, in the moments that matter. The AI mapping engine is technically interesting, but if the suggestion took 4 seconds instead of 2, or if the buttons were asymmetric, or if the original phrase was not preserved — the product would fail in ways no model improvement could fix. I want to study these moments with the rigour they deserve.

03 · FLAGSHIP CASE STUDY · 2025–PRESENT

Anatomy of a score.

Reducing a thirty-minute interview to a single number is a media problem before it is an algorithmic one.

Project
Kellafotey · AI hiring platform · Celloscope Ltd
Role
AI Product Manager · System designer
Year
2025 — present
Modality
Audio · Video · Transcript NLP
Performance
85%+ inter-rater agreement vs human evaluators

The Question

A hiring manager has nine candidates and forty-five minutes. She wants a score. The temptation, the easy product decision, is to give her a single number from 0 to 100 and let her sort. The honest design question is: what does that number have to be made of, and how should it be shown, so that it is fair to the candidate and useful to the manager?

Design Decisions

Integrity (cheat detection) is a multiplier, not a sixth dimension. If we showed it as a separate score, hiring managers would obsess over it; a candidate flagged for ambiguous integrity signals would be effectively rejected on that one number. Instead, we fold it into the final score as a 0.5–1.0 factor — visible if you click into the breakdown, but not the headline. This was a deliberate political choice, not a UI choice.

Behavioural and presentation signals (eye contact, posture, clothing) are weighted only 20% — and never reported in isolation. The temptation in multimodal AI is to report everything because you can. We chose not to surface the eye-contact-percentage number to the hiring manager at all; it feeds into the dimension score and stops there. A candidate's face is not a feature.

Every score is paired with evidence. The number is shown alongside two or three excerpts from the candidate's actual interview that drove the score. This forces the LLM to ground its judgement in transcript text, and it gives the hiring manager something to interrogate rather than just accept.

Technical Approach

Communication delivery uses ASR confidence, filler-rate detection, and prosody analysis (pitch jitter, amplitude variance) to produce sub-scores for clarity, fluency, structure, and confidence. Behavioural analysis uses gaze tracking and pose-keypoint variance over the interview duration. Content and knowledge runs an LLM scoring rubric against a per-question evaluation prompt. Master prompts for question generation and scoring achieved 85%+ inter-rater agreement with human evaluators, validated against a sample of 200+ hand-scored interviews.

What it Taught Me

Designing Kellafotey forced me to confront a question I did not have language for: what is the ethics of compression? Reducing a person to a score loses information by design. The question is not whether to do it (the manager has nine candidates and forty-five minutes); it is what to lose, what to keep, and how to make the loss visible. This is a media question. It belongs to the discipline I want to study.

05 · MSC THESIS · 2023

Forecasting non-stationary signals

SARIMA · Prophet · Comparative

Comparative analysis of two time-series models for cryptocurrency price forecasting using historical Bitcoin data, 2018–2023. SARIMA outperformed Prophet substantially: RMSE of 5,441 vs 27,992. The methodological lesson — that complexity is not the same as accuracy, and that classical models often beat fashionable ones on the right problem — has shaped how I evaluate ML systems at work.
